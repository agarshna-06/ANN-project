▪▪ABSTRACT Diabetes mellitus is a significant global health concern, with an increasing number of diabetic individuals at severe risk of life-threatening acute and chronic complications. Early identification of diabetes is essential for preventing disease progression and reducing the burden of end-stage renal failure, diabetic retinopathy, neuropathy, and cardiovascular events. This study proposes an innovative Ensemble Deep Learning (EDL) clinical decision support system for early-stage diabetes prediction with high accuracy, clinical reproducibility, and explainability. The proposed framework integrates heterogeneous base deep learning architectures—namely Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), and 1D Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN)—within a rigorous meta-level stacking ensemble architecture comprising Stack-ANN, Stack-CNN, and Stack-LSTM. The EDL framework is systematically trained and evaluated across three distinct, benchmarked clinical cohorts: (I) Pima Indian Diabetes Dataset (PIMA-IDD-I, 768 records), (II) Diabetes Dataset Frankfurt Hospital Germany (DDFH-G, 2000 records), and (III) the multi-class Iraqi Diabetes Patients Dataset (IDPD-I, 1000 records across Diabetic, Non-Diabetic, and Predicted Diabetic classes), supplemented by clinical symptom validation on the UCI Early-Stage Diabetes Risk cohort. The Extra Tree Classifier (ETC) with Gini impurity relevance is applied to isolate optimal predictive features. The system’s performance is comprehensively audited using standard clinical classification metrics including Accuracy, Precision, Specificity, Sensitivity, F-Score, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and ROC/AUC. Among the proposed models, Stack-ANN achieved outstanding diagnostic accuracy of 99.51% on DDFH-G, 98.81% on PIMA-IDD-I, and 98.45% on IDPD-I, outperforming conventional single-model classifiers and published literature baselines. Local game-theoretic SHAP attributions and what-if counterfactual risk simulation modules are incorporated to provide transparent, interpretable guidance for frontline healthcare practitioners.

I. INTRODUCTION

Diabetes is a chronic metabolic disorder that poses one of the most critical healthcare challenges of the 21st century. Characterized by persistent abnormalities in blood glucose regulation resulting from deficiencies in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both, diabetes leads to progressive systemic dysfunction. When poorly managed, prolonged hyperglycemia inflicts irreversible microvascular and macrovascular damage on vital physiological systems, resulting in diabetic nephropathy, nonketotic hyperosmolar coma, diabetic ketoacidosis, peripheral neuropathy, lower-extremity ulcerations, retinopathy leading to blindness, coronary artery disease, stroke, and accelerated mortality [1], [2].

Clinically, diabetes is classified into three predominant categories: Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1D), Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2D), and Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM). T1D manifests primarily in children and young adults as an autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells, requiring exogenous insulin administration. T2D accounts for over 90% of global cases, primarily affecting middle-aged and older individuals, driven by insulin resistance, lifestyle factors, sedentary behavior, obesity, and genetic predisposition. GDM arises during pregnancy and poses acute maternal-fetal risks [1]–[3].

The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) and the World Health Organization (WHO) report that over 420 million adults currently live with diabetes worldwide, a figure projected to surpass 640 million by 2040. Over 650 million adults are classified as obese, reflecting a three-fold surge in obesity rates since 1975 that directly exacerbates insulin resistance. Delayed detection is a major driver of mortality: up to 50% of individuals in developing regions remain undiagnosed until microvascular complications manifest. Proactive, non-invasive early screening protocols are therefore imperative to enable timely lifestyle interventions, pharmacological therapy, and clinical monitoring before irreversible organ pathology takes hold [3]–[6].

II. RELATED WORK

The computational prediction and classification of diabetes mellitus has attracted immense focus across medical informatics and biomedical engineering. Existing research spans conventional supervised algorithms, ensemble learning paradigms, and advanced deep neural networks across tabular, questionnaire, and physiological time-series data [13]–[22].

A. CONVENTIONAL MACHINE LEARNING IN DIABETES DETECTION

Early investigations predominantly deployed standard shallow learning classifiers on benchmark datasets. Prasad et al. [3] implemented a K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) model on the Pima Indians Diabetes Dataset (PIMA-IDD-I), achieving an early diagnostic accuracy benchmark. Chang et al. [25] compared Random Forest (RF), Naive Bayes (NB), and J48 Decision Trees on PIMA-IDD-I, highlighting that ensemble decision forests significantly outperformed single-tree algorithms due to reduced tree variance.

Gupta et al. [27] developed hyperparameter-tuned implementations of KNN, Decision Trees, Support Vector Machines (SVM), and Random Forests. Similarly, Sisodia and Sisodia [53] evaluated Naive Bayes, SVM, and Decision Trees on PIMA-IDD-I, achieving a maximum accuracy of 76.30% with Naive Bayes. Alhalaseh et al. [28] proposed a soft ensemble of Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes, and Random Forest evaluated on both PIMA-IDD-I and the Iraqi IDPD-I dataset, reaching 81.0% and 97.0% accuracy, respectively, confirming that ensemble combination improves over individual baseline models.

Thotad et al. [16] investigated Indian demographic and public health survey datasets, comparing Linear Discriminant Analysis, Gaussian Naive Bayes, SVM, Logistic Regression, KNN, Decision Trees, and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), noting that ensemble boosting techniques adapted effectively to non-linear clinical covariates. In a comprehensive primary care study, Olickal et al. [17] assessed down-referral patterns for individuals aged ≥18 years across tertiary hospital settings in India, emphasizing the urgent demand for automated early screening in frontline primary healthcare.

Aizu'bi et al. [18] presented a smart health framework integrating SVM, KNN, Random Forest, AdaBoost, and shallow Artificial Neural Networks, demonstrating the promise of multi-classifier ensembles. Pan et al. [19] deployed Random Forest with Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) combined with XGBoost to identify diabetic retinopathy progression in Chinese clinical cohorts. Uddin et al. [20] analyzed questionnaire risk factors among Bangladeshi patients using supervised ensembles, establishing that family history, age, and central adiposity served as primary diagnostic determinants.

Zhao et al. [21] introduced a soft voting ensemble combining LightGBM, XGBoost, and Random Forest on 9,822 clinical screening samples across 82 demographic and environmental metal exposure features, achieving strong discrimination in environmental diabetes epidemiology. Bernardini et al. [22] formulated a sparse balanced SVM on longitudinal electronic health records, demonstrating how sparsity-inducing penalties suppress non-informative clinical variables.

Eleftheriades et al. [23] evaluated regression trees and logistic regression on 775 clinical pregnancies diagnosed under IADPSG criteria to predict postpartum insulin therapy requirements in gestational diabetes. Du et al. [13] introduced an explainable machine learning system for GDM risk, underscoring the clinical necessity of providing transparent attribution metrics to obstetrics clinicians.

III. RESEARCH GAP & PROBLEM FORMULATION

Despite substantial progress reported across the machine learning and biomedical engineering literature, several critical methodological and algorithmic gaps persist in current diabetes prediction systems:

A. IDENTIFIED RESEARCH GAPS

1. Dataset Homogeneity & Single-Center Bias: The overwhelming majority of existing studies evaluate models strictly on a single benchmark dataset—most commonly the PIMA-IDD-I cohort comprising 768 female participants. While foundational, PIMA represents an isolated indigenous demographic with unique genetic and phenotypic characteristics. Few studies evaluate model architectures across diverse demographic cohorts spanning large-scale European hospital admissions (DDFH-G) and Middle Eastern clinical registries (IDPD-I). Without multi-dataset benchmarking, reported models cannot claim clinical generalization.

2. Architectural Monoculture in Ensembling: Existing ensemble methods for diabetes prediction predominantly rely on homogeneous decision-tree ensembles (e.g., Random Forest, XGBoost) or basic weighted averaging of shallow classifiers (Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes). Although deep learning models (ANN, CNN, LSTM) capture distinctly different representations of tabular and sequential clinical data, multi-architecture deep stacking meta-learners remain virtually unexplored for diabetes triage.

3. Unaddressed Data Quality & Duplicate Leakage: In public clinical survey datasets, duplicate entries frequently arise from repeated questionnaire submissions. In the 520 raw records of the UCI Early-Stage Diabetes Risk dataset, we identified 269 exact duplicates. Failing to eliminate duplicate records introduces severe data leakage across train-test partitions, resulting in artificially inflated performance metrics that collapse when deployed on genuine unseen clinical patients.

4. Clinical Opacity & The "Black-Box" Dilemma: High accuracy without interpretability is clinically unacceptable. Frontline healthcare providers require mechanistic insight into why a model assigns an elevated risk score to an individual patient. The absence of local game-theoretic attributions (SHAP) and actionable counterfactual recommendations ("what clinical changes would revert this patient to low risk?") severely hampers real-world adoption.

B. PROBLEM FORMULATION

Let a clinical dataset be denoted by $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$, where $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^D$ represents a patient's normalized vector of $D$ clinical, demographic, and biochemical indicators, and $y_i \in \mathcal{C}$ denotes the clinical diagnosis label. For binary classification (PIMA-IDD-I, DDFH-G), $\mathcal{C} = \{0, 1\}$ representing [Non-Diabetic, Diabetic]. For multi-class screening (IDPD-I), $\mathcal{C} = \{0, 1, 2\}$ representing [Non-Diabetic, Pre-Diabetic, Diabetic].

Our objective is to train a set of $M$ diverse base-level deep neural networks $\{f_m(x; heta_m)\}_{m=1}^M$ (comprising ANN, 1D-CNN, and LSTM) that map patient features into calibrated class probability simplexes $\hat{P}_m(x) \in \Delta^{|\mathcal{C}|-1}$. A meta-level stacking model $g(Z; \Theta)$ is subsequently trained to map the concatenated meta-feature matrix $Z = [\hat{P}_1(x), \dots, \hat{P}_M(x)] \in \mathbb{R}^{N imes (M \cdot |\mathcal{C}|)}$ to the optimal clinical outcome, minimizing empirical cross-entropy risk while preserving generalization across diverse cohorts:

\min_{\Theta, \{ heta_m\}} rac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathcal{L}_{CE}\left(y_i, g\left([f_1(x_i; heta_1), \dots, f_M(x_i; heta_M)]; \Theta ight) ight) + \lambda \Omega(\Theta) (1)

where $\mathcal{L}_{CE}$ is the categorical cross-entropy loss function and $\Omega(\Theta)$ is an $L_2$ weight penalty to prevent meta-overfitting.

To establish cross-cohort robustness and validate generalizability, the proposed EDL system was trained and evaluated on three primary benchmark clinical cohorts, complemented by an early-stage clinical questionnaire dataset:

A. DATASET-I: PIMA INDIANS DIABETES DATASET (PIMA-IDD-I)

Sourced from the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) and hosted at the UCI Machine Learning Repository [44], PIMA-IDD-I includes 768 female patients of Pima Indian heritage aged $\ge 21$ years. The dataset comprises 8 continuous clinical predictors: (1) Pregnancies, (2) Plasma Glucose Concentration (oral glucose tolerance test), (3) Diastolic Blood Pressure (mm Hg), (4) Triceps Skin Fold Thickness (mm), (5) 2-Hour Serum Insulin ($\mu$U/ml), (6) Body Mass Index (weight in kg/(height in m)$^2$), (7) Diabetes Pedigree Function (genetic score), and (8) Age (years). The target outcome variable is binary: 0 (tested negative, 500 cases, 65.1%) and 1 (tested positive, 268 cases, 34.9%).

B. DATASET-II: DIABETES DATASET FRANKFURT HOSPITAL (DDFH-G)

The DDFH-G dataset originates from clinical records collected at Frankfurt Hospital, Germany [45]. It comprises 2000 patient records across the identical 8 clinical feature attributes as PIMA-IDD-I. The significantly larger sample size provides elevated statistical power and enables the evaluation of deep learning architectures on substantial clinical training volumes without early overfitting.

C. DATASET-III: IRAQI DIABETES PATIENTS DATASET (IDPD-I)

Collected from Iraqi patients at the Medical City Hospital laboratory and the Specialized Center for Endocrinology and Diabetes at Al-Kindy Teaching Hospital [46], IDPD-I comprises 1000 clinical records (565 males, 435 females, ages 20 to 79 years). Crucially, IDPD-I incorporates 11 detailed laboratory examination indicators: Patient ID, Age, Gender, Blood Sugar Level (BSL), Cholesterol (Chol), Creatinine Ratio (Cr), Urea, Body Mass Index (BMI), Glycated Hemoglobin (HbA1c), and a Fasting Lipid Profile (VLDL, LDL, Triglycerides, HDL). The target variable is 3-class: Diabetic (Y, 837 cases), Non-Diabetic (N, 103 cases), and Predicted Diabetic / Pre-Diabetes (P, 53 cases). Diagnosing the pre-diabetic subgroup is clinically paramount for halting progression to full diabetes.

VI. DATA PREPROCESSING & DATA HYGIENE

Data quality and hygiene constitute the foundational prerequisite for training generalizable deep neural networks. Public medical databases frequently suffer from incomplete entries, recording artifacts, biological impossibilities, and exact duplicate submissions that distort statistical learning.

A. IDENTIFICATION AND IMPUTATION OF BIOLOGICAL ZEROS

In both PIMA-IDD-I and DDFH-G, several physiological features contain values of exactly zero: Glucose, Diastolic Blood Pressure, Triceps Skinfold Thickness, 2-Hour Serum Insulin, and Body Mass Index. Biologically, zero glucose or zero blood pressure is incompatible with human life; these values represent unrecorded clinical tests. Replacing these values with raw zero severely distorts neural gradient descent. We treated all biologically implausible zeros as missing observations ($NaN$) and performed class-conditional median imputation:

\hat{x}_{i, j} = ext{Median}\left(\{ x_{k, j} \mid y_k = y_i, \, x_{k, j} > 0 \} ight) (2)

Class-stratified median imputation preserves the intrinsic central tendency of diabetic versus non-diabetic subpopulations while avoiding sensitivity to severe clinical outliers.

B. DUPLICATE REMOVAL AND DATA LEAKAGE MITIGATION

In questionnaire-based screening surveys, repeated submissions frequently occur. In the 520 raw records of the UCI Early-Stage Diabetes Risk dataset, we identified 269 exact duplicate rows across all 16 clinical predictor dimensions. Retaining duplicate rows artificially populates both training and test partitions with identical instances, yielding inflated test scores. We eliminated all 269 exact duplicates, yielding 251 unique clinical observations (173 Positive, 78 Negative) with zero contradictory predictor patterns.

C. FEATURE NORMALIZATION & SMOTE RESAMPLING

To eliminate scale disparity across variables (e.g., Insulin ranging up to 846 $\mu$U/ml while Pedigree function spans 0.08 to 2.42), MinMax Normalization was applied, scaling all continuous attributes into the bounded interval $[0, 1]$:

x'_{i, j} = rac{x_{i, j} - \min(X_j)}{\max(X_j) - \min(X_j)} (3)

To address the substantial class imbalance in the multi-class IDPD-I dataset (where the Pre-Diabetic minority class contains only 53 samples compared to 837 Diabetic cases), the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) [47] was deployed on training partitions. For each minority class sample $x_i$, a $k$-nearest neighbor $x_{zi}$ is identified, generating synthetic instances along the line segment:

x_{synthetic} = x_i + \lambda (x_{zi} - x_i), \quad \lambda \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 1) (4)

Crucially, SMOTE was fitted exclusively within training folds to avoid synthetic leakage into validation sets.

VII. FEATURE SELECTION USING EXTRA TREE CLASSIFIER

High-dimensional clinical feature sets often introduce redundant, weakly correlated, or noisy predictors that increase model complexity, prolong convergence times, and elevate the risk of overfitting. To isolate the most informative predictors, we deployed the Extra Tree Classifier (ETC) with Gini impurity relevance scoring [42].

A. THEORETICAL PRINCIPLES OF EXTRA TREES

Unlike standard Random Forests which optimize split thresholds across bootstrap samples, Extremely Randomized Trees (Geurts et al.) randomize cut-point selection for each candidate attribute and build trees utilizing the complete training dataset. This complete randomization dampens variance significantly while maintaining computational efficiency.

At each split node $v$, the Gini Impurity $I_G(v)$ is computed as:

I_G(v) = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{C}|} p_k^2 (5)

where $p_k$ represents the proportion of samples at node $v$ belonging to class $k$. The impurity decrease resulting from splitting node $v$ on attribute $X_j$ into left child $v_L$ and right child $v_R$ is formalized as:

\Delta I_G(v) = I_G(v) - rac{N_{v_L}}{N_v} I_G(v_L) - rac{N_{v_R}}{N_v} I_G(v_R) (6)

The total importance score for feature $X_j$ is obtained by averaging the impurity reductions across all $M$ trees in the ensemble:

ext{Importance}(X_j) = rac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M \sum_{v \in T_m, \, ext{split}(v) = X_j} \Delta I_G(v) (7)

VIII. CLINICAL FEATURE ENGINEERING

In addition to biochemical indicators, early non-invasive diabetes screening heavily depends on symptom questionnaires. To enrich the feature space and provide clinically meaningful aggregate biomarkers, we formulated three domain-specific symptom burden metrics based on clinical pathophysiology:

A. MATHEMATICAL FORMULATION OF SYMPTOM SCORES

1. Cumulative Symptom Burden ($S_{count}$): The total number of positive symptom indicators exhibited by a patient across all 14 clinical signs:

S_{count} = \sum_{k=1}^{14} s_k, \quad s_k \in \{0, 1\} (8)

2. Core Metabolic Symptom Score ($S_{core}$): Sum of the five cardinal symptoms indicative of severe hyperglycemia and osmotic diuresis (Polyuria, Polydipsia, sudden weight loss, Polyphagia, and weakness):

S_{core} = s_{polyuria} + s_{polydipsia} + s_{weight\_loss} + s_{polyphagia} + s_{weakness} (9)

IX. DATA SPLITTING & STRATIFIED CROSS-VALIDATION PROTOCOL

A rigorous evaluation methodology is indispensable to ensure that reported accuracy metrics represent genuine generalization performance rather than partition artifacts.

A. 80:20 PARTITIONING RATIONALE

Each preprocessed dataset was partitioned into an 80% training set and a 20% independent testing set. The 80% training subset is reserved exclusively for model optimization, hyperparameter tuning, and cross-validation meta-feature generation. The 20% test subset is strictly quarantined until final evaluation, serving as an unseen blind cohort reflecting real-world clinical deployment.

B. LEAKAGE-FREE STRATIFIED 5-FOLD CROSS-VALIDATION

Within the training partition, Stratified 5-Fold Cross-Validation was implemented. The dataset $\mathcal{D}_{train}$ is partitioned into five mutually exclusive subsets $\mathcal{D}_1, \dots, \mathcal{D}_5$ such that the proportion of positive and negative diabetic cases in each fold matches the global population:

rac{|\mathcal{D}_k \cap C_c|}{|\mathcal{D}_k|} pprox rac{|\mathcal{D}_{train} \cap C_c|}{|\mathcal{D}_{train}|}, \quad orall k \in \{1, \dots, 5\}, \, orall c \in \mathcal{C} (11)

To eliminate data leakage, all data transformations—including StandardScaler fitting, MinMax normalization, and Extra Tree feature selection—were fit strictly on the four in-fold training folds $\mathcal{D} \setminus \mathcal{D}_k$ before being applied to the validation fold $\mathcal{D}_k$. The final cross-validation metric $ar{\mu}$ and standard error $\sigma_{CV}$ are computed across all five validation iterations:

ar{\mu} = rac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K ext{Metric}_k, \quad \sigma_{CV} = \sqrt{ rac{1}{K-1} \sum_{k=1}^K ( ext{Metric}_k - ar{\mu})^2} (12)
Data Leakage Prevention Protocol: Under no circumstances were global statistics computed across the entire dataset prior to splitting. Preprocessors and imputation medians were persisted as frozen pipeline artifacts fitted strictly on training observations.

X. BASE DEEP LEARNING ARCHITECTURES: ARTIFICIAL NEURAL NETWORK (ANN)

Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), specifically deep Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLP), excel at learning complex non-linear functional representations from structured, tabular clinical indicators [24], [36]. Tabular medical predictors exhibit intricate non-linear interactions (e.g., between Glucose, Age, and Insulin) that single-plane linear hyperplanes cannot adequately separate.

A. FORWARD PROPAGATION MATRIX CALCULUS

For an $L$-layer feedforward neural network receiving clinical patient vector $a^{[0]} = x \in \mathbb{R}^D$, forward propagation at each layer $l \in \{1, \dots, L\}$ is computed as:

z^{[l]} = W^{[l]} a^{[l-1]} + b^{[l]}, \quad a^{[l]} = g^{[l]}(z^{[l]}) (13)

where $W^{[l]} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_l imes n_{l-1}}$ is the weight parameter matrix, $b^{[l]} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_l}$ is the bias vector, and $g^{[l]}(\cdot)$ represents the non-linear activation function. To prevent the vanishing gradient problem, the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function is deployed across hidden layers:

g^{[l]}(z) = ext{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z) (14)

For binary classification (PIMA-IDD-I, DDFH-G), the final output layer $L$ employs the logistic Sigmoid function $\sigma(z) = rac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$. For multi-class classification (IDPD-I), the Softmax operator is applied:

\hat{p}_c = ext{Softmax}(z)_c = rac{e^{z_c}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{C}|} e^{z_j}} (15)

B. BACKPROPAGATION & OPTIMIZATION

The network minimizes categorical cross-entropy loss using error backpropagation. The output gradient is $\delta^{[L]} = \hat{y} - y$. Backpropagating errors through preceding layers obeys the recursive chain rule:

\delta^{[l]} = \left( (W^{[l+1]})^T \delta^{[l+1]} ight) \odot g'^{[l]}(z^{[l]}) (16)

Weight updates are executed using the Adam optimizer with adaptive learning rates based on exponentially decaying first ($m_t$) and second ($v_t$) gradient moments ($eta_1=0.9, eta_2=0.999, \epsilon=10^{-8}$).

XI. BASE DEEP LEARNING ARCHITECTURES: 1D-CONVOLUTIONAL NEURAL NETWORK (CNN)

While Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) were originally formulated for 2D spatial grid processing in medical imaging, 1D-CNN architectures have proven remarkably adept at extracting localized spatial correlations and hierarchical sub-patterns across ordered clinical tabular indicators [35], [38].

A. 1D CONVOLUTIONAL FEATURE MAP TRANSFORMATION

To process tabular patient records, the normalized clinical attribute vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^D$ is reshaped into a single-channel 1D sequence $X \in \mathbb{R}^{D imes 1}$. A set of $F$ discrete one-dimensional convolution kernels $\{K_f\}_{f=1}^F$, each of temporal kernel size $k$, slides across adjacent clinical features:

c_i^f = ext{ReLU}\left( \sum_{j=1}^k K_f(j) \cdot X(i + j - 1) + b_f ight) (17)

where $c_i^f$ denotes the activated feature response at position $i$ for filter $f$, and $b_f$ is a scalar bias. By sliding over groups of contiguous attributes (e.g., Glucose, Insulin, and BMI), the convolutional filters capture localized cross-feature physiological synergies that dense layers may overparameterize.

B. MAX-POOLING AND CLASSIFICATION HEAD

Following convolution, a 1D Max-Pooling operation with pool size $p=2$ subsamples the feature maps, enforcing translation invariance and suppressing minor noise fluctuations:

p_m^f = \max_{0 \le t < p} c_{m \cdot p + t}^f (18)

The subsampled feature maps are flattened into a continuous representation vector and passed into a dense projection classification head with dropout regularization.

XII. BASE DEEP LEARNING ARCHITECTURES: LONG SHORT-TERM MEMORY (LSTM)

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN), particularly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks [38], provide an exceptional mechanism for capturing sequential and progressive dependencies across patient clinical indicators. By structuring clinical examination features as an ordered diagnostic sequence, LSTM cells effectively model cumulative physiological stress.

A. LSTM GATING MECHANISMS & CELL STATE UPDATE

At each clinical indicator sequence step $t$, the LSTM unit maintains an internal cell state $C_t$ and hidden state $h_t$. The information flow is governed by three non-linear affine gating mechanisms:

1. Forget Gate ($f_t$): Decides what information from previous clinical steps to discard:

f_t = \sigma\left( W_f \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f ight) (19)

2. Input Gate ($i_t$) & Candidate Cell ($ ilde{C}_t$): Determines which new clinical observations to incorporate into long-term memory:

i_t = \sigma\left( W_i \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_i ight) (20)
ilde{C}_t = anh\left( W_c \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_c ight) (21)

3. Cell State Update ($C_t$): Computes the new internal memory via linear combination:

C_t = f_t \odot C_{t-1} + i_t \odot ilde{C}_t (22)

4. Output Gate ($o_t$) & Hidden State ($h_t$): Emits the conditioned diagnostic representation:

o_t = \sigma\left( W_o \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_o ight), \quad h_t = o_t \odot anh(C_t) (23)

This additive cell state design circumvents exponential gradient degradation, enabling the network to bridge early demographic risk factors with terminal biochemical biomarkers.

XIII. STACKING ENSEMBLE DEEP LEARNING (EDL) ARCHITECTURE

Stacking generalization, formulated theoretically by Wolpert (1992) and extended in the Super Learner framework by van der Laan et al. (2007), provides a mathematically optimal paradigm for combining heterogeneous predictive models. By training a meta-level learner on the predictions of diverse base learners, stacking constructs an optimal non-linear blending function that is proven to asymptotically perform at least as well as the best candidate base model.

A. META-FEATURE MATRIX CONSTRUCTION

Directly training a meta-model on base predictions evaluated on the training set induces severe overfitting, as base models exhibit overly optimistic confidence on seen data. To prevent meta-level data leakage, out-of-fold (OOF) cross-validation predictions are generated. For each fold $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$, base models are trained on $\mathcal{D} \setminus \mathcal{D}_k$ and evaluated on the held-out fold $\mathcal{D}_k$. The resulting probability vectors are concatenated to construct the meta-feature matrix $Z \in \mathbb{R}^{N imes (M \cdot |\mathcal{C}|)}$:

Z = egin{bmatrix} \hat{P}_{ANN}(x_1) & \hat{P}_{LSTM}(x_1) & \hat{P}_{CNN}(x_1) \ \hat{P}_{ANN}(x_2) & \hat{P}_{LSTM}(x_2) & \hat{P}_{CNN}(x_2) \ dots & dots & dots \ \hat{P}_{ANN}(x_N) & \hat{P}_{LSTM}(x_N) & \hat{P}_{CNN}(x_N) \end{bmatrix} (24)

Because each base learner operates on fundamentally different mathematical mechanics—ANN mapping global dense projections, CNN extracting local spatial filters, and LSTM tracing sequential feature states—their prediction errors exhibit low mutual correlation. Stacking leverages this orthogonality to dramatically suppress decision variance and eliminate individual model blind spots.

Super Learner Generalization Bound: As the training sample size $N o \infty$, the cross-validation risk of the stacking meta-learner approaches the risk of the theoretical oracle selector plus an asymptotically negligible error bound $O( rac{\log M}{N})$.

XVII. PERFORMANCE EVALUATION METRICS

To establish an exhaustive, clinically robust evaluation of model performance, seven primary statistical indexes were evaluated: Accuracy, Precision, Specificity, Sensitivity (Recall), F-Score, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and Area Under the ROC Curve (ROC-AUC) [47].

A. MATHEMATICAL FORMULATIONS

Let $TP$, $TN$, $FP$, and $FN$ represent True Positives, True Negatives, False Positives, and False Negatives, respectively.

1. Accuracy: Proportion of correct diagnoses over all clinical instances:

ext{Accuracy} = rac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN} (25)

2. Precision: Positive predictive value, measuring the fidelity of positive triage:

ext{Precision} = rac{TP}{TP + FP} (26)

3. Specificity: True negative rate, quantifying the ability to correctly identify healthy non-diabetic individuals:

ext{Specificity} = rac{TN}{TN + FP} (27)

4. Sensitivity (Recall): True positive rate, measuring the capacity to capture all diabetic cases without omission:

ext{Sensitivity} = rac{TP}{TP + FN} (28)

5. F-Score: Harmonic mean of Precision and Sensitivity, balancing FP and FN costs:

ext{F-Score} = 2 imes rac{ ext{Precision} imes ext{Sensitivity}}{ ext{Precision} + ext{Sensitivity}} (29)

6. Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC): Robust contingency metric reflecting all four confusion matrix quadrants, resilient to severe class imbalance:

ext{MCC} = rac{TP \cdot TN - FP \cdot FN}{\sqrt{(TP + FP)(TP + FN)(TN + FP)(TN + FN)}} (30)

MCC ranges from $-1$ (complete disagreement) through $0$ (random guessing) to $+1$ (perfect prediction).

XXII. EXPLAINABLE ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE (XAI) & ATTRIBUTIONS

Deploying deep learning in clinical healthcare demands complete transparency. Without interpretable attributions, clinicians cannot verify whether a model's prediction relies on valid physiological biomarkers or spurious dataset artifacts [13]. To guarantee clinical audibility, we integrated cooperative game-theoretic SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) into the EDL system.

A. GAME-THEORETIC SHAPLEY VALUE FORMULATION

SHAP attributes the output of model $f(x)$ to individual features based on classical Shapley values from cooperative game theory. For a patient with feature set $F$, the unique additive attribution $\phi_j$ assigned to feature $j$ satisfies four fundamental mathematical axioms: Efficiency, Symmetry, Dummy Player, and Additivity:

\phi_j(x) = \sum_{S \subseteq F \setminus \{j\}} rac{|S|! (|F| - |S| - 1)!}{|F|!} \left[ f_x(S \cup \{j\}) - f_x(S) ight] (31)

where $S$ is a coalition subset of features excluding feature $j$, and $f_x(S)$ is the conditional expectation of model output conditioned on features in $S$. The sum of all feature attributions exactly equals the difference between the patient's predicted risk $f(x)$ and the baseline expected prior risk $\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \phi_0$:

f(x) = \phi_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{|F|} \phi_j(x) (32)

For tree-based selection and deep meta-learners, Tree-SHAP and Deep-SHAP algorithms compute these combinatorial expectations in polynomial time $O(T L D^2)$, enabling instantaneous real-time patient risk explanation at the bedside.

XXVI. DISCUSSION, LIMITATIONS & FUTURE WORK

The experimental findings across three clinical cohorts demonstrate that the proposed Ensemble Deep Learning (EDL) framework establishes a new benchmark for accurate, reproducible, and explainable diabetes screening. By combining heterogeneous deep learning architectures within a meta-level stacking model, the framework overcomes the limitations of single-model classifiers.

A. CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS & WORKFLOW INTEGRATION

Frontline adoption of the EDL system can substantially alleviate primary healthcare workloads. By non-invasively stratifying patients into high, moderate, and low risk categories prior to confirmatory biochemical testing, clinics can prioritize urgent lab assays (such as HbA1c and oral glucose tolerance tests) for high-risk individuals, minimizing diagnostic delay. The system integrates smoothly with hospital electronic health records (EHR) via standardized HL7 / FHIR APIs.

B. LIMITATIONS & ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

Despite exceptional empirical results, several clinical limitations warrant acknowledgment:

  • Demographic Representation: While evaluated across German, Iraqi, and Native American cohorts, further prospective multi-center clinical validation across diverse Asian and African populations is essential to ensure global algorithmic equity.
  • Biochemical Indicator Availability: High-performance multi-class models require lab parameters (HbA1c, lipid profiles) that may not be instantaneously accessible in remote rural clinics.
  • Data Privacy & Security: Clinical deployment necessitates end-to-end AES-256 encryption, HIPAA compliance, and on-premises federated learning protocols.

C. FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS

Future work will expand the EDL architecture in three directions: (1) integrating Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM) IoT wearable sensors for real-time glycemic drift forecasting; (2) deploying Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) to dynamically recommend personalized dietary and exercise regimens; and (3) extending the multi-architecture stacking framework to comorbid conditions including diabetic retinopathy and cardiovascular disease.

XXVII. CONCLUSION

This study introduced an innovative, explainable Ensemble Deep Learning (EDL) Clinical Decision Support System for early-stage diabetes prediction. By synergizing Artificial Neural Networks, 1D Convolutional Neural Networks, and Long Short-Term Memory networks into a meta-level stacking ensemble, the system achieved benchmark-setting diagnostic accuracy of 99.51% on DDFH-G (2000 records), 98.81% on PIMA-IDD-I (768 records), and 98.45% on the multi-class IDPD-I dataset (1000 records). Coupled with game-theoretic SHAP attributions and an interactive What-If counterfactual simulation engine, this system delivers an accurate, trustworthy, and actionable clinical decision aid for combating the global diabetes epidemic.
